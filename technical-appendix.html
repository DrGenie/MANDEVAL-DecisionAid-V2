MandEval – Technical appendix

Understanding the MandEval vaccine mandate tool

This appendix explains how each tab in the MandEval tool works, how the results are calculated and how they should be interpreted. It is organised in two parts:

  • Part A – Non-technical overview for policy and programme audiences.
  • Part B – Technical details for analysts and researchers.

Part A – Non-technical overview

1. Inputs from the discrete choice experiment (DCE)

The tool is based on a multi-country DCE carried out in Australia, France and Italy. Members of the public chose between alternative vaccine mandates and a “no mandate” option. Each mandate was described using:

  • Scope – high-risk workers only, or everyone in public spaces.
  • Exemptions – medical only; medical + religious; or medical, religious and personal-belief.
  • Coverage requirement – mandate lifted at 50%, 70% or 90% coverage.
  • Expected lives saved – 10, 25, 35 or 40 lives saved per 100,000 people.
  • Outbreak framing – “mild” or “severe” epidemic context.

Statistical models (mixed logit and latent class logit) summarise how people trade off these attributes when deciding whether they support a mandate.

2. How the tool is structured

Introduction
Explains the purpose of the tool and the overall workflow.
Mandate design features
Shows the attributes and levels used in the DCE, with simple icons and plain-language descriptions.
Settings & assumptions
Lets you specify the evaluation period (e.g. 3 years), the number of people exposed to the mandate and the value per life saved (using country-specific default VSL values, with the option to override).
Configuration
Lets you select the country, outbreak scenario (mild / severe) and the mandate design: scope, exemption rules, coverage to lift the mandate and expected lives saved per 100,000 people. When you click Apply configuration, the tool:
  • Computes model-based predicted support for the mandate.
  • Computes total lives saved, benefits and costs.
  • Updates the headline recommendation and results tab.
Costing details
Contains editable cost components, such as set-up and IT costs, administration and enforcement, and communication costs. These are expressed per person or per million people and are scaled using the population and evaluation period.
Results
Shows key indicators (lives saved, predicted public support, benefits, costs, net benefit and BCR) and three charts:
  • A bar showing predicted public support.
  • A bar chart comparing total benefits and total costs (in millions).
  • A horizontal bar chart showing “lives-saved equivalents” for each design feature.
It also includes an interpretation box that explains these outputs in plain language and a briefing text that can be copied directly into emails or reports.
Saved scenarios & exports
Lets you save multiple configurations, compare them in a table and export decision-ready summaries as PDFs or Word documents. You can also copy an AI prompt and open Copilot or ChatGPT in a new window to generate narrative interpretations.

3. How to read the results

Predicted public support tells you whether the mandate is politically and socially feasible.
Benefit–cost ratio (BCR) tells you whether the health benefits are large enough to justify the costs on economic grounds.
Lives-saved equivalents tell you how strongly people care about design features relative to lives saved.

Example (non-technical):

Suppose you configure a mandate in France under a severe outbreak that covers all occupations and public spaces, allows only medical exemptions, and is lifted at 90% coverage. You set expected lives saved at 35 per 100,000, the exposed population at 5 million people and use the default French VSL.

The tool might indicate that around 70–75% of people are predicted to support this design, that it saves several hundred lives over three years, and that the monetary benefits are several times larger than the implementation costs (BCR well above 1). The lives-saved equivalents show that broad coverage and stricter exemptions are valued almost as much as additional lives saved, suggesting that people care strongly about fairness and effectiveness, not just about the number of lives saved.

Part B – Technical details

4. Model-based public support

For each country and outbreak framing, the tool uses the mean coefficients from a mixed logit model estimated in preference space. The utility of a mandate alternative is:

U_M = α_A + β_scope * I(scope = all) + β_exMR * I(ex = medical+religious) + β_exMRP * I(ex = medical+religious+personal) + β_cov70 * I(cov = 70%) + β_cov90 * I(cov = 90%) + β_lives * L

where L is the expected lives saved per 100,000 people. The utility of the “no mandate” option is:

U_O = α_opt-out

The tool then computes the probability that a mandate is chosen over the opt-out option as:

P(support) = exp(U_M) / (exp(U_M) + exp(U_O))

These probabilities are reported in percentage form and presented as “model-based public support”. They are conditional on the selected design and outbreak scenario.

5. Benefits and costs

Let:

  • p be the exposed population.
  • T be the evaluation period in years.
  • L be expected lives saved per 100,000.
  • V be the value per life saved (VSL basis).

Total lives saved are computed as:

Total lives saved = L × (p / 100,000) × T

Monetary benefit is:

Benefit = Total lives saved × V

Costs are specified as a combination of:

  • C_setup – one-off set-up and IT cost per 1,000,000 people.
  • C_admin – administration and verification per person per year.
  • C_enforce – enforcement and inspection per person per year.
  • C_comms – communication and engagement per person over the entire period.
  • C_other – other mandate-related costs per person over the entire period.

The tool converts these into a per-person cost over the period:

C_per_person = (C_setup / 1,000,000) + (C_admin + C_enforce) × T + C_comms + C_other

Total cost is:

Total cost = C_per_person × p

Net benefit and BCR are then:

Net benefit = Benefit − Total cost
BCR = Benefit / Total cost

6. Lives-saved equivalents (MRS)

At the model level, the lives-saved equivalent (marginal rate of substitution) for a design attribute k is:

MRS_k = −β_k / β_lives

In the tool, two related quantities are reported:

  • Attribute-level lives-saved equivalents – for each country and scenario, the above ratios are computed for: scope, exemptions and coverage. These are shown in the horizontal bar chart.
  • Configuration-level lives-saved equivalent – the tool compares your chosen design with a benchmark (targeted high-risk, medical-only, lifted at 50% coverage) by summing the relevant β-coefficients and dividing by βlives.

The configuration-level value is interpreted in the results tab as “your chosen design is equivalent to increasing or reducing expected lives saved by X per 100,000 people compared to the benchmark”. The actual formulas are kept in this appendix; the main interface only presents intuitive explanations.

7. Worked examples

Example 1 – Changing scope

In the Australian severe scenario, the mixed logit estimates have β_scope(all) = 0.190 and β_lives = 0.079. The lives-saved equivalent of moving from “high-risk only” to “all occupations and public spaces” is:

MRS_scope = −β_scope / β_lives ≈ −0.190 / 0.079 ≈ −2.4

This means that, on average, people value broadening the scope of the mandate as much as an increase of about 2.4 expected lives saved per 100,000 people.

Example 2 – Configuration-level lives-saved equivalent

Suppose in Italy under a mild outbreak you choose: “all occupations & public spaces”, “medical + religious exemptions” and “lift at 70% coverage”. The configuration-level change in deterministic utility (relative to the benchmark) is:

ΔV = β_scope(all) + β_exMR + β_cov70
with β_scope(all) = −0.276, β_exMR = −0.176 and β_cov70 = 0.185.

So ΔV ≈ −0.267. With β_lives = 0.039, the configuration-level lives-saved equivalent is:

LivesEq = −ΔV / β_lives ≈ 6.8 lives per 100,000.

The tool reports this as “people would require roughly 7 extra lives saved per 100,000 to be as satisfied with this design as with a targeted, medical-only, 50% threshold mandate”.

8. Limitations

  • The public support estimates are based on stated preferences at one point in time and may differ from revealed preferences in future epidemics.
  • Cost inputs are deliberately simple and need to be replaced with detailed national costing where available.
  • Values per life saved are based on VSL estimates and may not capture distributional or equity concerns; these should be discussed alongside the quantitative results.
  • Extrapolating the lives-saved attribute outside the original 10–40 per 100,000 range should be done with caution.

Despite these limitations, the MandEval tool provides a transparent way to link public preferences, epidemiological outcomes and economic reasoning into a single, communicable framework for mandate design.
